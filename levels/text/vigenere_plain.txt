For most of history people who wanted to keep a message secret relied on simple tricks. A general might replace each letter with the letter three places later in the alphabet and hope that the enemy would not notice the pattern. That worked only as long as the enemy did not think about it for very long. Once someone realised that the same letter was always replaced by the same symbol, the secret was gone, because some letters are used far more often than others. In English the letter E appears in almost every sentence, followed by T, A, O, I and N. A patient reader who counts the symbols in a long message can match the most common symbol to E and slowly rebuild the rest of the alphabet.

The cipher that people later named after Blaise de Vigenere was meant to defeat this kind of counting. Instead of one shift for the whole message, it uses a keyword, and each letter of the keyword gives a different shift. The first letter of the message is moved by the first letter of the key, the second by the second, and when the key runs out it simply starts again from the beginning. Because the same plain letter can now become several different cipher letters, the simple counts look flat, and for almost three hundred years many writers called it the indecipherable cipher.

The weakness is hidden in the words starts again. A key that repeats creates a rhythm. If the same word appears twice in the message at a distance that happens to be a multiple of the key length, it is encrypted in exactly the same way both times. In the nineteenth century Charles Babbage and later Friedrich Kasiski noticed that these repeated fragments reveal the length of the key. Measure the distances between repeats, look for the number that divides most of them, and you have a strong guess. A second method compares how often letters coincide when the text is lined up against itself. Natural language has a high index of coincidence, while random letters have a low one, so the shift that produces the highest value tells you the period.

Once the length of the key is known the rest is almost mechanical. Write the ciphertext in columns as wide as the key. Every letter in the first column was moved by the same amount, so that column is nothing more than an ordinary Caesar cipher, and the old method of counting letters works again. Find the most common letter in each column, assume it stands for E, and read off the shift. Put the shifts together and the keyword appears. With the keyword in hand the whole message can be decrypted in seconds, and the secret that once seemed safe is laid out in plain view.

The lesson that modern designers took from all of this is that a cipher must never let the structure of the language leak through. A good cipher should make the output look like random noise even to someone who knows exactly how the algorithm works. Only the key should be secret. This idea is often called the principle of Kerckhoffs, and every serious system built today follows it.
